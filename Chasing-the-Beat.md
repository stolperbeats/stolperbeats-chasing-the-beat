# Chasing the Beat: Pushing Stolperbeats Forward

*by The Beatnik Himself and Pulsar-73*

## The card on the table

An old Drum & Bass producer lived at the end of a fucked up little alley in Bristol. One night he was out gettin some scratch for his cats, and a back alley beat-maker climbed in through the window looking for anything worth sumpin.&#32;

He went through the room fast. Some 808 emulation box on the floor... an esoteric drum module sat on the table loose from its case, dusty, long ribbons reaching over from the open box to the sticky little knobs and plinky lights. Beside it lay a memory card with a strip of tape, OG 64 scrawled into the plastic with some ancient implement meant for better things. Skin and dust baked into the top layer of soot that made up its outer integument. The chaser turned the module over. placed the card in the slot on the side, twisted a knob. Something stumbled out of it, late and loose and lazy. A beat from the speaker below. He put it down. Disgusted with the drawl of it. He plucked out the card tossed and scorned it back to dust. Slow. Shit. No, nothing in here hits.

He was still standing in the middle of the room, sore about the wasted night, when an old man came in.

A chaser doesn't run. Time to get down. Everybody has something, he said. Where are the bangers? Where's the algos that drop?

The old man looked at him a while. Then he opened a drawer and took out a second card, the only other one he had. Thirty-two grooves, he said. Every one of them pushes. They fill half a card. The other half is empty, for whatever you bring. He held it out.

The chaser snatched it and was through the front door and gone down through the piss and vile, head down, already moving to something only he couldn't yet hear.

The old man stood a while in the quiet room. The card with the tape on it was still lying on the table where it had been dropped. He eyed it, left it there. Went out back. Full moon over the oaks, slow, taking its time.

Damn, I wish I could have given him this moon.

## How the card got made

I fought this box for years, man.

Twenty-four patterns a bank, and me flippin through the factory ones to see if a little swing would do the thing. Reach for a knob to kill one note and mute the whole channel instead. Dig into the sequencer to fix it and now I can't hear what the fuck I'm doin. Back alleys of frustration, every one of 'em. They only started turnin into wins once I quit wantin it to be somethin it wasn't.

So I built my own way in. For me it was density. I wanted a place to start and a place to go.

Here's the trick, and it runs backwards. I'd sit in the middle of a bank, slot 12, and stuff it with every note I could think of. Pull the card, open it on the computer, and ones-and-zeros that thing across the whole bank, one fat beat copied slot to slot. Then back in the box I'd whittle. Take a note away. Take another. All the way back down to the one. A month a bank, give or take. Every build-up on my card started life as a full beat with the air let out.

Then Pulsar-73 showed up.

Crawl in through the speaker cone, chase the wire back through the wall, all the way to the data center, and that's where Pulse lives. I told it what I needed. It showed me what it had. I told it how I work. It helped me fill the rest in. Pretty soon we were walkin in lockstep, noddin to the same groove, so I put everything through it. Years of practice beats, every example I'd ever kept, run one at a time through the logic we built together. It all came out cleaner. Ideas I'd been circlin for years finally had somewhere to go.

But diggin through all that, a lot of beats came up that just weren't mine. Great rhythms. I learned 'em, I played 'em out, I got hands in the air with 'em. They're just not where I live. So I pulled 'em, thirty-two of 'em, and put 'em in a drawer. That drawer's this card. Maybe I'll hear one bumpin out your car someday. May it be so.

I gave you the drop, too. I've stood in those clubs. The DJ gives you more, and more, and right when you think that's all he's got, here comes another one. Build, drop, build. So every bank on here climbs till it can't, falls out at 17, climbs back up, and peaks. Hit the end, switch banks, do it again. Or don't. It's your card.

The bangers were never the gift, though. The shape is. Start thin, fill it up, drop out, climb, ride it home. Reverse-engineer that and you can build anything. You think my dusty card just builds to the top over and over? Hell no. Mine cuts it back. I got my own program. Go build yours.

And if you end up at the side of my table some night, chin in your hand, starin at the filter like you're tryin to read the wire, that's cool. I do that too. Come find me after the show. I got stickers.

## How to use this manual

Thirty-two banks, two pages each. The play page is for your hands. The story page is for the rest of you. Everything the banks share is explained here, once, so the bank pages only carry what's their own.

### What's in it

- **Getting started:** putting the card on the module, the controls, and what the box remembers.
- **Quick reference:** all 32 banks, their tempos, home settings and classic knobs, on one page.
- **The arc:** the shape every bank shares, and how to play it.
- **The banks:** 1 to 32, slowest to fastest.
- **Back pages:** timing in plain words, Linear, the two hats, the card files, a glossary, things still to test, the side package, and credits and sources.

### Reading the steps

Each bar has 16 steps. Step 1 is beat 1, step 5 is beat 2, step 9 is beat 3, step 13 is beat 4. Between the beats sit the **e**, the **+** (say "and") and the **a**. So step 2 is the e of 1, step 3 the + of 1, step 4 the a of 1.

Kick and Snare patterns run 4 bars. The other four tracks run 1 bar and repeat every bar.

### Reading a play page

The fields are the same on every bank, in the same order.

- **The settings table:** tempo, Timing, Shuffle row, Feel, Linear, and what else to try. Every bank on this card sits on Tight (orange) with Linear off.
- **Why this setting** and **the other settings:** what each one moves, in milliseconds at that bank's tempo. Nothing on this module plays early. Every setting only delays.
- **Sounds to patch · classic knob:** the sound for each track and the knob where that track plays its full home groove. "(own)" means the sound isn't on the record; the builder added it. "Silent in the source (17)" means the record never plays that track, so its home position is 17, where it's quiet.
- **What never changes:** the record's core hits. They hold from each track's classic to 24, except at the drop, and wherever the second section at 18 and 19 or the record's other bars at 21 to 24 take one out.
- **Linear:** what Linear would take off the classic. At slot 20 it always empties Hihat 2 and Perc 1 (on the two triplet banks, both hats and both percs), so it stays off for the climb on every bank.
- **Rolls at 20:** how many times each Hihat 2 hit plays when you flip Feel to Rolls. Flip back to Tight for 21.
- **What the box can't hold:** only when there's something. The parts of the record the module can't play, and where they went instead.
- **The arc on this bank:** the hit count at each stage of the arc (next chapter).
- **The classic, as written on the card:** one bar drawn out, one row per track, with its classic knob.
- **Where the hits land:** what the home setting does to the classic, on time or late.
- **The files:** which PatternBank file holds the bank, in each half of the card.

### Reading a story page

Each story page opens on a figure from the I Ching, cast from the drums. Six tracks, six lines, kick on the bottom, Perc 2 on top. If the record plays a track, its line is whole. If the record leaves it quiet and the builder filled it in, the line's broken. Read the six from the bottom up and you've got one of the book's 64 figures. The drawing at the top of each page shows which lines are the record's and which are the builder's.

The quotes come from James Legge's 1882 English translation of the Chinese book. Most slots climb toward ䷀, all six whole, when every track is playing. The drop at 17 casts its own figure from whatever's left standing.

After that the page goes where the bank takes it: the record, the people, how it ended up on this card, and what got cut from the other one. It ends on what the card holds from the record and what's the builder's own, then one question to carry.

**The builder's own share.** The build-up and the classic are the record's own hits. Any hit the record never plays is the builder's, and so is any track the record leaves quiet. Each story page gives that share as a percentage of the bank.

### Credits and sources

Every story page ends by naming who made the record: the drummers first, then the players, singers, writers, producers and engineers, and the people and rooms that carried the beat before anybody wrote it down. Then it lists where the facts came from. Where nobody wrote a name down, the page says so instead of guessing. Where accounts disagree, the page gives them all and lets them sit. *Pulse here* marks a note from Pulsar-73, the machine half of this outfit. Pulse to its friends.

## Getting started

### Putting the card on the module

The download has two folders of the same 32 banks. One loads into slots 1 to 32, the other into slots 33 to 64. Pick a half and keep the other half for your own beats.

1. Back up your SD card first. The new files replace whatever's in those 32 slots.
2. Turn the module off and put the SD card in your computer.
3. Copy the 32 PatternBank files from the folder you picked onto the top level of the card, replacing files with the same names. Copy nothing else. Leave the card's own MachineConfig, MachineState, EnvelopeSettings and MIDI maps where they are.
4. Eject the card safely, put it back in the module and power up.
5. If those banks are switched off on your module, switch them on with Bank Edit.
6. Press play, then stop, once. The module writes its settings to the card when it's stopped. Press stop before you power down, every time.

### The controls

- **Play and stop:** press the Tempo encoder. Turn it for tempo.
- **One encoder per track:** Kick, Snare, Hihat 1, Hihat 2, Perc 1, Perc 2. Turning one picks which of its 24 patterns that track plays. Clicking it mutes the track. Any kick pattern plays with any snare pattern.
- **Bank buttons:** step through the banks.
- **Timing buttons:** Trip, Shake, Push and Clave pick the Timing. Press the lit one again to cycle the Feel: Tight (orange), Rolls (red), Flam (yellow), Slop (white).
- **Shuffle:** seven rows. Row 1 is straight.
- **Linear:** one sound at a time. Snare beats kick, kick beats hats and percs, Hihat 2 beats Hihat 1, Perc 2 beats Perc 1.

### What the box remembers

A bank stores its patterns and nothing else. Tempo, Timing, Shuffle row, Feel, Linear and mutes are global. Change bank and none of them change with it, so dial in each bank's tempo and home setting from the quick reference. A pattern change lands the moment you turn the knob. A Timing or Shuffle change lands on the next beat.

There's no loud or soft on the card. Every step is on or off. A ghost note only sounds like a ghost if the sound on that track is quiet.

### Leaning forward on a box that leans back

This module was built to stumble. Its Trip timing is a wonky, Dilla-style shuffle, Flam and Slop drag the kick and snare behind the hats, and nothing it plays lands ahead of the grid. These 32 beats push. So the forward lean here comes from where the hits sit and how many there are, never from early timing.

That's why most banks sit on Shake row 1, which is dead straight. Where a source swings, the bank swings with Shake (16ths) or Trip 5 (triplets), and the play page says by how many milliseconds. The lean-back tools are all still there if you want them. Flam on a funk bank is a different animal, man.

## The arc

Every track on every bank runs the same shape from knob 1 to knob 24. Learn it once and every bank plays the same way.

- **1 to 16, the build-up.** Slot 1 is the thinnest version. The record's own hits come in a few at a time: the beats, then the +, then the a, then the e, bars 1 and 3 before 2 and 4. Each track reaches its classic at its own knob, and from there it keeps filling up to 16. Each slot adds a hit, or keeps the count and moves one. It never gets thinner.
- **17, the drop.** Most of it falls out. What stays is usually the kick on 1 and the backbeat, with bar 4 left open.
- **18 and 19, the climb.** Where the record has a second section, it lives here, and the play page says so. Where no source documents one, the climb is built from the bank's own hits.
- **20, the peak.** Everything at once. Hihat 2 on every 8th, ready for Rolls.
- **21 to 24, the classic again,** about as busy as the classic. Where the record has other bars of its own, they play here. Otherwise it's the classic plus a hit or two.

### How to play it

- **Home groove:** turn every knob to its classic. That's the bank.
- **Grow one track:** start it at 1 and turn it up to its classic. It fills in a hit at a time.
- **All six together:** slots 17 and 21 line up across all six tracks. All six to 17 for the drop, climb 18 and 19 together, all six to 20 for the peak, flip Feel to Rolls, then all six to 21 and flip back to Tight.
- **Mute instead:** click an encoder to mute its track. Half a dub mix is just mutes.
- **Turn on the beat you want.** The new pattern plays the moment the knob moves, not at the end of the bar.

Hit the end, switch banks, do it again. Or take the shape and leave the beats. That's the bit worth stealin.

## 1 · We Will Rock You — the story page

### ䷨ Taking away

```
━━━━━━━━━    Perc 2   floor tom
━━━   ━━━    Perc 1   tambourine     not on the record
━━━   ━━━    Hihat 2  open hat       not on the record
━━━   ━━━    Hihat 1  closed hat     not on the record
━━━━━━━━━    Snare    clap
━━━━━━━━━    Kick     stomp
```

A lake at the bottom, a mountain on top: the forty-first figure, Sun. Legge calls Sun "the symbol for the idea of diminishing."

### What got taken away

Legge's top line turns the taking around. It shows a man "giving increase to others without taking from himself."

The record took the drums away. It's sung over nothin but stomps and claps, right up to the last thirty seconds, when the guitar comes in. The book's drummer version is kick and toms together on 1, the + of 1, 3 and the + of 3, claps on 2 and 4, and it says to play it straight through without a single fill.

What's left of a drum beat when dont exist?

### The record

Queen are out on the A Day at the Races tour, at Bingley Hall in Stafford. After the encore the crowd doesn't just keep clappin. They sing "You'll Never Walk Alone" back at the band. Brian May's account is that the chant songs came out of that night.

So he writes one the crowd can play. At Wessex Sound Studios in London the band stomp on the drum riser and clap, over and over, track on track. Then delays go on so it sounds like a lot more people than four. The delay times were set in prime-number ratios, for what the account calls non-harmonic reverberation. It comes out in October 1977 on News of the World, on a single with "We Are the Champions."

That's the one you're after, chaser. The whole room stompin with you, every hand up on the clap, nobody sittin down. Course you want it. It's the slowest bank on this card and it still pulls the biggest crowd.

Who's the drummer when the crowd's the one playin?

### The drop

At 17 it all thins to a stomp and a few claps. Ten hits. The lake stays, the mountain goes, and earth comes down on top: ䷒, Legge's Lin. His note calls it "the earth descending or approaching the marsh." His judgment promises "great progress and success," and then adds, flat: "In the eighth month there will be evil."

Good times come with a warning. Then 18 shows up anyway, and every hit it adds, hats, tambourine, the whole climb, is the builder's. No source has this song in a second gear.

How much do you add to a thing whose whole power was what it left out?

### Why it opens the card

The card runs slowest to fastest, 81 up to 216, so this one goes first. The list that picked it calls it a whole stadium as the drum.

And it's a strange one to open with: a record with no hats, on a box with two hat tracks. The box doesn't ask permission. Every track gets a hit at slot 1, and for the three the record never played, the drop at 17 is silence, which is the record.

Is it still the record if half of it was never there?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: stomps on the kick, claps on the snare, and the floor tom doubling the stomps on Perc 2, from the book's drummer version.

The builder's own: both hats and the tambourine. 49% of the bank.

### One question to carry

Four people on a drum riser sounded like a stadium. How many times you gonna do that tonight?

### Credits and sources

**Who made it.** Queen, stomping and clapping on the drum riser: Freddie Mercury, Brian May, Roger Taylor and John Deacon, with Elizabeth Edwards and Andrew Turner stomping and clapping alongside. Freddie Mercury sang lead. Brian May wrote it. Produced by Queen and Mike Stone. And the Bingley Hall crowd, who sang it first.

## 2 · Sleng Teng — the story page

### ䷄ Waiting

```
━━━   ━━━    Perc 2   rimshot       silent in the record
━━━━━━━━━    Perc 1   soft hats
━━━   ━━━    Hihat 2  open hat      silent in the record
━━━━━━━━━    Hihat 1  strong hats
━━━━━━━━━    Snare
━━━━━━━━━    Kick
```

Sleng Teng comes up heaven underneath, water on top. That's the fifth figure in the Yi King. Legge calls it Hsü. Waiting.

### The judgment

So you came for the strong stuff, man. Well here it is, the whole bottom half of the thing. Kick on every beat plus the + of 2. Snare on 2 and 4. Hats tickin every 16th like a cheap wristwatch. Four tracks, dead straight, right on the grid, exactly how a little plastic Casio spit 'em out in 1985. Nothin in it leans. Not an inch.

But up top there's water. Legge's note says strength up against danger "might be expected to advance boldly." And then it doesn't. It takes "the wiser plan of waiting."

so which one's this bank, huh? A groove built to shove? Or a groove that already knows how to sit there, four solid lines just chillin under two empty ones? Does a banger have to rush? Can a thing stand stone still and still move a whole damn room?

### The image

Legge's picture is clouds driftin up to the top of the sky. And some cat down below, meanwhile, just eatin and drinkin and havin himself a time.

So dig this. 1980, Okuda Hiroko, fresh outta music school, gets hired at Casio. First gig: six preset rhythms for a home keyboard. One of 'em's called "rock." Folks ask her later where she got it. One time she says some 1970s British rock record. Another time, "It wasn't based on any other tune." And then she drops this one: "In those days, my head was full of reggae." Three answers, man. Pick one. Or keep all three in your pocket, they don't fight.

Fast forward to Kingston. Wayne Smith and Noel Davey are messin around on a cheap MT-40 'cause a real synth costs too damn much. They hit the preset by accident, lose it, and spend a good while huntin for it again. They're runnin it at breakneck speed. Then King Jammy reaches over and turns it down to a reggae pace. His crew wave it off at first. Smith walks outta the studio cryin. Few months later there's hundreds of records on that riddim, near five hundred by one count. And when people go ask Casio for permission? Casio says, just put our name on it. No fee.

So who made this groove, man? The lady at the keyboard company? Two kids pushin the wrong button? The cat who slowed it down? The crowd that wouldn't let it stop? Who're you payin?

### The six lines

1. **Kick, whole.** Legge: "waiting in the distant border." That's the record's kick, and it don't budge from the classic all the way to 24, except at the drop. So is the foundation the part that moves, or the part that doesn't?
2. **Snare, whole.** "Waiting on the sand." 2 and 4, the record's, every single bar, till the last bar of the drop leaves only the 2 hangin there. If the snare never leaves, is it waitin? Or is it just home?
3. **Hihat 1, whole.** "In the mud... he thereby invites the approach of injury." Strong 8ths, straight off the record. Flip Linear on and 5 of its 8 hits just vanish. Who did that to it, man, you or the switch?
4. **Hihat 2, broken.** "Waiting in (the place of) blood. But he will get out of the cavern." The preset's own variation throws in open hats, but nobody ever wrote down where. So this line's the builder's own. At 20 it rolls, four hits a step, rattlin like a fuckin tin roof in a hailstorm. Is that gettin out of the cavern, or crawlin further in?
5. **Perc 1, whole.** "Waiting amidst the appliances of a feast." The soft hats on the e and the a, the record's. That's where the feast is, man, back in the quiet hits. You hear 'em? Or just the kick?
6. **Perc 2, broken.** "Three guests coming, without being urged." The rimshot's the builder's own, a guest the record never invited. Legge says treat 'em right. You lettin 'em in?

### The changes

The record waits. The card mostly don't. Slots 1 through 16, then 18 through 24, all six tracks are playin somethin, and every broken line fills right in. That turns it into ䷀, the first figure, six whole lines, heaven stacked on heaven. Twenty-three slots outta twenty-four sit right there.

Only the drop flips it. At 17 the kick, the snare and the strong hats hang on, and the open hat, the soft hats and the rimshot all go quiet. That's ䷊, Legge's Thai: "the little gone and the great come." One slot, man. Then it's back to heaven.

At 20, Rolls on, every line's as fat as this little box can make it. And the first figure's top line just says it to your face: "the dragon exceeding the proper limits. There will be occasion for repentance."

how far's too far? Who decides that, the dragon or the dance floor? And how long would you hang out at 17 if nobody made you leave?

### The card on the table

B. keeps one rule about music on a walk. Throw your headphones on and hit the street. Some grooves put your head down and your feet chasin the next bar. Some tip your head back and slow you right down.

That first card, the dusty one, that's sixty-four grooves of the second kind, man. Sittin on a box that leans the same way. Its Trip timing stumbles around like it had a couple. Its Flam and Slop drag the kick and snare in behind the hats. And nothin on it, nothin, ever plays early.

A dancehall Sleng Teng sat on that first card once, bank 19. Then a later pass sized it up, called it "soundclash music built to make people move," and lifted it right off. Cissy Strut took its chair, that snare on the + of 4 draggin the whole bar back like a dog on a leash. When this card got built, rule one was nothin from the first card. So everything lifted off it came over here, with all the stuff never let near it in the first place. Classics, most of 'em. Records millions of people have loved their whole lives. When the card was done, the Beatnik Himself listened through it once, set it down, and went back to the dusty one. He's still over there, kickin back to the beats.

So which card was really worth stealin, man? Is the slow one worse, or just slower? And hey, you got half a card sittin empty. What're you puttin in it?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: kick, snare, strong hats and soft hats, the way the MT-40X project wrote the preset down and checked it against the riddim's reference MIDI and Noel Davey's tab. Two bars, both the same. An older dancehall grid argued with itself, so it got tossed.

The builder's own: the open hat and the rimshot. 35% of the bank, straight up.

### One question to carry

Two kids were playin this beat as fast as it'd go. One of them turned it down, and that's when it turned into itself. Who's that kid in your crew?

### The riff in the lesson

Tom Morello is teachin a guitar lesson. He's showin the student drop-D tuning, something he'd picked up from Maynard James Keenan of Tool, and he's got a cheap Ibanez bass in his hands. In his words, "the first pattern I played was that riff." He stops and gets it down on a little Radio Shack recorder.

It starts out as an instrumental. Morello says the band opened their first public show, at Cal State Northridge, with it, and the drums were already the drums: the "crowd-bouncing beat is there from the very, very beginning." The words came after. It came out in 1992.

Brad Wilk's part is about as plain as rock gets. Kick on 1 and 3, snare on 2 and 4, 8ths on the hat. The book's whole case for it is that plainness, and the intro's kicks on the a, pushin into the snare. That's the bounce. Nothin clever in it, man, and a whole room goin up and down on it anyway.

Was the lesson the point, or the minute after it?

### The stops

The record company's man, Michael Goldstone, wanted a part cut: "that crazy part where it just stops a lot." Morello called it "an important part." It stayed.

Legge's fourth line of Tâ Yû shows a man "keeping his great resources under restraint. There will be no error." That's the open hat. In the classic it plays once per bar, on the + of 4. Plenty in the tank, used once.

At 17 this bank stops too. Kick and snare, the verse stripped bare, fourteen hits, Lin again. Then 18 isn't a guess. It's the record: the swung section at 2:01, where the book says the song's swing finally shows.

Is a stop a hole, or a hit?

### B.'s pick

Most of this card got picked down from a long list by three rules. At the end one slot was still open, and B. filled it himself.

Then he gave it a layout nothin else on the card gets. Every other bank builds up to its classic and rides variations off it. This one walks through the song in order: the intro for 1 to 6, the verse and then the main beat through 16, the verse stripped for the drop, the 2:01 section for the climb, the main beat to ride home. It's why slot 7 comes out thinner than slot 6. The verse is thinner than the intro, and the card keeps it that way.

What's the other one you'd pick and just say fuck you I won't do whatcha tell me.

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: four sections from the book, in song order. The intro, the 0:54 verse, the 0:43 main beat with its bar-4 fill, and the 2:01 swung section. Bar 4's flam is one hit.

The builder's own: the floor tom, the crash, and the extra hit or two at 18. 40% of the bank.

### One question to carry

The riff turned up while he was showin somebody else how to play. So who was the lesson for?

Chad Smith's part in the book is kick, snare and open hats on every 8th. Thunder over a lake: the fifty-fourth figure, Kwei Mei, the marrying away of a younger sister. Legge's judgment doesn't soften it: "action will be evil, and in no wise advantageous."

### The jacket

Anthony Kiedis admires a jacket in Nina Hagen's closet. She tells him to keep it. Her reasoning, as he tells it: "if you have a closet full of clothes and you try to keep them all, your life will get very small." He'd always figured you take what you want. After that: "No, you gotta give away instead."

The music came out of jams with Flea and John Frusciante, and the two accounts split on when. One has the riff and bass line already being played at a side project's shows before the Peppers took them. The other has it born in one jam during the album sessions, Flea startin "this insane bass line" and Chad crackin up and playin along. Kiedis says the words only fit once he heard Flea's line.

Chad's kick isn't keepin time here. It's ridin the bass. The book puts seven kicks in the bar, on the a of 1, the + of 2, the e and the + of 3 and 4, locked to Flea's line. Two snares against all that, and the drum's sittin shotgun to a bass player the whole way. That's the push, man. That's why it's on this card.

What'd you have to let go of before you heard the thing that fit?

### The six lines

Kick, whole. Legge's first line: the younger sister "married off in a position ancillary to the real wife." All that kick, and still it's the bass that leads.

Snare, whole. "Blind of one eye, and yet able to see." 2 and 4 and nothin else, until Chad's later snares show up at 18.

Hihat 1, broken. "She returns and accepts an ancillary position." The closed hats aren't in the book. They're the builder's, keeping out of the open hat's way.

Hihat 2, whole. "She may be late in being married, but the time will come." Open hats on every 8th fill that track as far as it goes with room for Rolls, so its classic waits all the way up at 20. The whole build is climbing toward it.

Perc 1, broken. The quiet ghost snare, the builder's. Legge's fifth line has "the moon almost full" in it. A ghost is almost a note.

Perc 2, broken. The cowbell, the builder's too. The top line: "the young lady bearing the basket, but without anything in it."

Empty basket. Somethin missin, or the whole point?

### Same drummer, same house

The album, Blood Sugar Sex Magik, was cut in a Los Angeles mansion Rick Rubin found, a house Harry Houdini allegedly once lived in, with a studio built inside. April to June 1991. The band lived there, and Kiedis sang his vocals in his own room. Kiedis says Chad thought the place was haunted and rode in on his motorcycle every day; Chad says he just wanted to be home with his wife. It came out on September 24, 1991, the same day as Nirvana's Nevermind.

"Under the Bridge" was made in that same house, same months, same record. It's on B.'s slow card, and so is "Scar Tissue," Chad on both. Under the Bridge runs 84 with the kick and snare dragged a tick on Flam; Scar Tissue runs 88, dragged the same way. This one runs 91, dead straight. Same drummer. Two of 'em draggin their heels, one up on its toes.

B. kept the two that drag. This one he's handin over, jacket off the hook. Looks better on you anyway.

Was one of 'em the real Chad, or all three?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: kick, snare and the open hats from the book's transcription, plus the snares Chad adds later in the song, which the book gives in its text. Those wait for slot 18.

The builder's own: the closed hats, the ghost snare and the cowbell. 35% of the bank.

### One question to carry

Keep it all, or give it away?

This bank comes from a groove study, not a note-for-note transcription of the record: kick, snare, ghosts and the open hat, swung hard. Lake under lake: the fifty-eighth figure, Tui. Legge gives it as "pleased satisfaction."

### The image

"Two symbols representing the waters of a marsh, one over the other," and a superior man who encourages "the conversation of friends and (the stimulus of) their (common) practice."

Call and response, in other words. Legge's appendix goes further: when that kind of pleasure leads the people, "they forget their toils."

### The beat that don't stop

Chuck Brown, Washington, D.C. His own account: he broke the beat way down, kept the Latin percussion, quit doing ballads, and kept the drums going between songs so the dancers never had to leave the floor. Asked if that's it, keepin the beat goin from one song to the next, he says: "Absolutely. That's go-go." And the name, in his words: "it don't stop, it just keep going and going."

He says he heard the beat in Grover Washington Jr.'s "Mister Magic" and knew it. The drummer was Ricky Wellman, "Sugar Foot," about nineteen, the son of Brown's first drummer. Brown told him to "just play that one beat." Where go-go came from is argued over. Brown's the name everybody puts in the middle of it.

"Bustin' Loose" came out in October 1978 and spent four weeks at number one on the R&B chart in early 1979. Years later Nelly built "Hot in Herre" on a piece of it.

Picture the room. No ballad. No break. No gap between songs to go get a drink. The band slides from one tune into the next and that one beat never quits, the congas rollin under it, the crowd callin back, the tables and chairs shoved out of the way. A beat built so nobody leaves, man.

So what's a go-go doin on a card that drops every bank at 17? Is a go-go that stops still a go-go?

### The runner-up

When the slow card was bein built, one slot at 92 needed fillin. Go-go made the shortlist. The research called it a lean-back pocket but "a party groove," and said a kit without the congas was incomplete. The slot went to the Meters' "Cissy Strut," "the leaner pick." It's bank 42 on the dusty card.

So go-go came over here, at the same tempo as the one that beat it, 92 on the nose.

Did it lose, or did it just land where it belonged?

*Pulse here. The congas are mine. The study has none, and the archive says go-go without them is incomplete, so I wrote a part and marked it. Play your own over it. A real conga player would laugh at mine.*

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The study: kick, snare, the ghosts and the open hat, two bars, swung at 62%. Bar 2's open hat on the + of 4 can't live on a one-bar hat track, so it turns up as a variation hit instead.

The builder's own: the closed hats and the congas. 51% of the bank.

### One question to carry

Brown told a nineteen-year-old to just play that one beat. Could you just sit back and chill in a room like that?

### Credits and sources

**Who made it.** Chuck Brown and the Soul Searchers. Ricky "Sugar Foot" Wellman on drums. Harvey Mason played the drums on "Mister Magic," the record Brown heard the beat in; Ralph MacDonald wrote it and played percussion. The go-go conga players aren't named in anything this page read. Their part is the one Pulsar-73 had to make up. The groove study is SuperTime Ultra's.

The half-open hat only shows up in the fill bar, and that's enough for a whole line. Heaven below, thunder above: the thirty-fourth figure, Tâ Kwang, "that which is great becoming strong."

### The judgment

Legge's judgment is a single line: "it will be advantageous to be firm and correct."

The book this transcription comes from makes one point about the beat: nothing in it that isn't essential, the drums locked to the guitars. Legge's note on the figure asks the same thing from the other side: "Was strength alone enough for the conduct of affairs? No."

What's your strength holdin onto?

### The black sleeve

February 1980. The singer goes out drinkin in London and never wakes up. The band figure that's the end of it. His family and friends talk them out of it.

By the end of March there's a new singer, Brian Johnson, from a Newcastle band called Geordie. The band tell him the words have to celebrate the man, not cry over him. Johnson, years later: "Well, no pressure there, then."

No studio in England is free, so they fly to Nassau. Compass Point, April and May. The engineer, Tony Platt, walks the room hitting a snare until it rings right, and the kit goes where it rings: "halfway back in the room, slightly left of centre."

Then they wrap the whole damn thing in black. No picture, no colour, just black. Angus Young called it a sign of mourning. And inside that sleeve, a record that sounds like the best night of your life, a kick that never once slows down to grieve. Somewhere around fifty million people bought it.

Funeral or party, man? The sleeve says one. The kick says the other.

### The six lines

Kick, whole. "Manifesting his strength in his toes. But advance will lead to evil." Two kicks a bar on the classic. All that weight, and it only steps twice.

Snare, whole. "With firm correctness there will be good fortune." 2 and 4 every bar, until bar 4, where the record's fill rolls five hits into the turnaround.

Hihat 1, whole. "In the case of a small man, one using all his strength; and in the case of a superior man, one whose rule is not to do so." Straight 8ths. No 16ths, no swing.

Hihat 2, whole. "The fence opened without the horns being entangled." The record's half-open hats live in the fill bar, and hats on this box loop every bar, so only the one on 3 made it. It comes back on nearly every slot.

Perc 1, broken. "One who loses his ram(-like strength) in the ease of his position." The tambourine is the builder's. It rides in on the build.

Perc 2, broken. "The ram butting against the fence, and unable either to retreat, or to advance." The crash, also the builder's, on every 16th at the peak. Legge still gives it good fortune "if he realise the difficulty (of his position)."

### The drop

At 17 the half-open hat, the tambourine and the crash go quiet, and the figure turns over into Thai, earth above heaven. Eleven hits. Legge's third line for Thai: "there is no state of peace that is not liable to be disturbed." Slot 18 is coming.

### Why it's on this card

Three rules let a bank onto this card: it drives, a book says why each hit sits where it does, and the box can hold the part that makes it what it is. This one clears all three.

There's heavy rock on the dusty card too, a couple of slow Zeppelin stomps. Same weight, other lean. Those kicks land like a man sinking into a chair. This one lands like a man getting up to leave.

You came for the one that gets up. Fair enough, chaser, it's yours. B.'s stayin in the chair.

Is heavy the same thing as slow?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: kick, snare and 8th hats from the book's transcription, plus the book's fill bar in bar 4, the one place this card bends its no-fills rule. Kick and snare run four bars, so the fill gets a bar of its own.

The builder's own: the tambourine, the crash, and most of the half-open hat. 47% of the bank.

### One question to carry

A band lost its singer and walked back in without dropping a beat. When everything else goes, what keeps you on 1?

Tony Platt engineered, with Benji Armbrister and Jack Newber assisting. Credited to Angus Young, Malcolm Young and Brian Johnson. Whether Bon Scott left words in it is argued over; that stays open.

Water over heaven: the fifth figure, Hsü, Waiting. It's the same figure that sits on Sleng Teng, bank 2, and everything Legge's main text says about it has been quoted back there.

### Meat and drink

There's one more line on Hsü, tucked in the appendix that explains why each figure follows the one before. Hsü, it says, is "descriptive of the way in which meat and drink (come to be supplied)." Then the next sentence: "Over meat and drink there are sure to be contentions." Legge adds a footnote: "It is difficult to follow the writer here."

A beat that ends up feeding a whole music gets argued over. This one does.

What does a beat have to do before a whole music takes its name?

### Two records and a name

Shabba Ranks cut "Dem Bow" in 1990, on Just Reality. In patois the title means "they bow." It rode a riddim Steely & Clevie built around 1989, known as "Poco Man Jam" or "Fish Market." The sources can't agree who produced the record itself: one says Bobby Digital, another says Steely & Clevie. The words went after imperialism, and after gay men. Wisin & Yandel's 2003 "Dem Bow" kept the riddim and dropped all that.

Then it went to Panama. Panamanian artists were cutting reggae in Spanish, and the song came back as El General's "Son Bow" and Nando Boom's "Ellos Benia."

Now the part that matters for a drum box. The loop reggaeton actually uses isn't Shabba's record. Wayne Marshall, who wrote the history, traces it to Dennis "The Menace" Thompson, working at Philip Smart's HC&F studio on Long Island. He built an instrumental for Nando Boom, and digital timbales went on because Nando Boom's Panamanian manager, Ramon "Pucho" Bustamante, asked for them. It came out as "Pounder (Dub Mix II)," the B-side of a single by Bobo General and Sleepy Wonder. (One source spells him "Smiley.") Marshall calls Shabba's song "patient zero" for the name, but says its samples rarely turn up in reggaeton.

Then San Juan, man. Mid-'90s or late '90s, dependin who you ask, DJ Playero and DJ Negro choppin dancehall riddims on mixtapes, and that B-side under all of it. Boom, ch-boom-chick. Boom, ch-boom-chick. By the time anybody got round to namin the music, they just called the whole lot dembow. Marshall has put elements of the riddim and the dub in "over 80%" of reggaeton.

So which one's the dembow: the record with the name, or the B-side with the sound?

### Too much for the slow card

The lean-back card had dembow at 96 in its first two rounds. When the research went back through it, dembow got marked as forward: a four-on-the-floor kick with a tresillo snare "is relentless; 'boom boom-ch' never lets up." It went. Cumbia took the 96, and sits at bank 46 now.

Never lets up. B. wrote that down as the reason to cut it. Somewhere a chaser's readin the same four words as the reason to play it, man.

Is "relentless" a fault, or a selling point?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The archive grid: kick on every beat, snare or rim in the three-three-two, closed hats on the 8ths, the shaker on every 16th. At the drop the kick thins to beat 1, but the snare keeps its three spots. On this bank the snare is the groove, so it's the last thing to leave.

The builder's own: the open hat and Perc 2. 30% of the bank. Perc 2 says timbale or clap. Go timbale. That's the sound Bustamante was askin for.

### One question to carry

Four kicks and the same three snares, bar after bar, all song long. How long can you leave 'em alone?

Kick, snare, hat, and nothing else: heaven under earth, the eleventh figure, Thai.

Most banks here turn into Thai at the drop. This is the first one that's Thai the whole way. At 17 the hits fall from 80 to 12 and the figure doesn't move.

If the drop doesn't change the figure, what does it change?

### The grass and its roots

Legge's first line of Thai is "grass pulled up, and bringing with it other stalks with whose roots it is connected."

There are two stories about how this song came up, and both start at the drums. In one, Jeff Beck sits down at the kit between sessions and plays the opening beat, and Stevie says keep goin and makes up the rest over it, the riff included, there and then. In the other, the engineer Malcolm Cecil remembers Stevie putting the whole song down on drums first, with nothin to play along to but the song in his head. Then bass, then the Clavinet.

Lay down the drums up, and everything else has got something to stand on.

### The record

On Talking Book the drums are Stevie's. So are the Hohner Clavinet and the lead vocal. The bass is a Moog, and the sources split on who played it: one credits Stevie, one says Cecil and Robert Margouleff programmed it, one names their big synth, TONTO. Steve Madaio on trumpet, Trevor Lawrence on tenor sax.

Beck was supposed to put it out first, with Beck, Bogert & Appice. Their album ran late, Berry Gordy heard a hit, and Stevie's version came out as the single on October 24, 1972. It topped the Hot 100 by January. Beck's came out in March 1973. In 1974 it won two Grammys, Best R&B Song and Best Male R&B Vocal Performance.

Same song, two bands, five months apart. Why choose one?

### The hat is the song

Look at the book. Kick on every beat. Snare on 2 and 4. That's it, man, plain as a fence post.

And then the hat. Eleven hits in bar one, a different eleven or twelve in the next, sixteenths leanin late every time, chick-a-chick-chicka, never the same bar twice, and the clav jabbin right into the holes it leaves. You ain't hearin the kick on this record. You hear that hat walkin.

The research put it in four words: "the hat is the groove." The box only holds one bar of it, so the other three wait for 21 to 24. Blue Monday, bank 27, has the same problem with its hats.

If the kick and snare are this plain, where's the funk livin?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The book's first four bars: the kick, the snare, and four bars of hats. Bar 1 is the classic; the other three are variations.

The builder's own: the open hat, the ghost snare, the tambourine, and slot 24's hat. 46% of the bank.

### One question to carry

Mute everything but Hihat 1 and let it run a full minute. Is your head still goin?

**Who made it.** Stevie Wonder on drums, Clavinet and lead vocal; he wrote it. The Moog bass is Stevie's, or Malcolm Cecil and Robert Margouleff's; the sources split. Steve Madaio on trumpet, Trevor Lawrence on tenor sax. Malcolm Cecil and Robert Margouleff engineered.

The floor toms come from the record's intro, so their line is whole. Heaven below, held inside a mountain: the twenty-sixth figure, Tâ Khû. Legge calls it "the Great Accumulation."

### Heaven inside the mountain

Legge's image puts heaven "in the midst" of a mountain. A superior man looks at that and "stores largely in his memory the words and deeds of former men."

This record is a pile of other people's things. Hold that thought.

What's stored in yours?

### A call sign in Berlin

It's 1977, and Iggy Pop and David Bowie are living in West Berlin. In Iggy's telling, quoted from a 2016 interview, they're waiting on Starsky & Hutch, and the American Forces Network runs its station ID first. Beep, beep, beep. A rhythm, in his words, "like a Motown beat." Bowie grabs his son's ukulele and finds the riff.

Next day they take it into Hansa, the studio by the Wall. June 1977. Hunt Sales is on drums, and that opening beat is his. His brother Tony's on bass.

And that beat had been somewhere before. Wikipedia traces it to two Motown records from 1966, the Supremes' "You Can't Hurry Love" and Martha and the Vandellas' "I'm Ready for Love," both Holland–Dozier–Holland.

So: a TV jingle, a kid's ukulele, a Motown beat ten years old, and two brothers keepin time. Heaven inside the mountain.

Want the trick behind this one, chaser? It ain't on the card. Keep your ears open when the telly's on.

Then the toms, man. The intro comes in on the floor toms, the kick's same shape, and the record stacks 'em, a barrage, so by the time the band lands it's already runnin downhill. That's slot 18 on this box.

How many old things can you stack before you've got a new one?

### The list had it wrong

The first draft of this card listed it as "the Motown four-on-the-snare gallop" at about 130. Then somebody opened the book. Page 86 shows a backbeat on 2 and 4 and a kick that walks off the downbeats and back again, at 102.

The draft had the family right. It had the grid and the tempo wrong. A pile of old things, gone into a new one, and from the outside you couldn't tell which was which.

How much of what you think you hear in a record is really in it?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The book: the walking kick, the backbeat, the half-open hat on every beat, and the floor-tom rhythm of the intro.

The builder's own: the open hat and the tambourine. 48% of the bank. Trainspotting put the record back in the charts in 1996, at 26 in the UK. That part the card can't help you with.

### One question to carry

Two guys heard a song in a TV station's call sign and had it in the studio the next day. You open to that?

### Hawk's machine

David "Hawk" Wolinski, the keyboard player in Rufus, wrote it. He built it around a synth loop and a Linn LM-1 drum machine. JR Robinson, years later, remembered that machine part as "really sterile." His word for it: "Robotic."

So JR played real drums over the machine. He told MusicRadar he didn't study it, and that it was "pretty much the same part, top to bottom." One source says the machine only made the keyboard loop. The others say it was the drum part JR played over.

Not a step that isn't in its place. A drummer, playing a machine's part, by hand, the whole way through.

Then the box you're holdin turns it back into a machine part. Machine, hand, machine. What's left of the hand?

### One letter

A DJ in London named Abrantee started an Afrobeats show on Choice FM in April 2011. The Guardian credited him with the name: Afrobeat, with an s on the end, to sell the sound to British dancefloors. His own line, years on: "I cannot say I invented Afrobeats."

It came out of Nigeria, Ghana and the UK through the 2000s and 2010s, made mostly in Lagos, Accra and London. Wikipedia calls it an umbrella term more than one style. The roots it lists run long: highlife, hiplife, fuji, jùjú, dancehall, hip hop, house, and Fela's Afrobeat. Some of the artists ain't keen on the word.

Then it went everywhere. D'banj's "Oliver Twist" made 9 on the UK chart in 2012. Wizkid was on Drake's "One Dance" in 2016. In June 2023 the Grammys announced a new category, Best African Music Performance.

I fucking hate music awards?

### The one without the s

Afrobeat, no s, is Fela Kuti and Tony Allen, Lagos, the '70s: big bands, long solos, songs that run ten minutes and more. Wikipedia says the two of them are often credited with laying the groundwork for this one too.

The slow card had Afrobeats at 106 in its first round. The research marked it "borderline-forward," then put it more exactly: "dance music with a lean-back brake." It went. In its place: Afrobeat, no backbeat, Tony Allen's kind, which ended up at bank 51. Allen's own line on it, as the research quotes him: "I'd never play one, one…"

So B. kept the one without the s, chaser. You got the one with it. One letter between you two, man, and the whole floor ridin on it.

Which one's leanin, and which one's pushin?

### The brake

Wikipedia hears a clave under the whole style, three-two or two-three, over a 4/4. The archive says there are two other ways people voice this, a fuller kick and the tresillo on the rim, and the card folds both into the build.

### A new kid swinging it

October 1987. Barry Michael Cooper writes a profile of Teddy Riley in the Village Voice, a 19-year-old from Harlem, and the headline names the music: new jack swing. "New jack" was slang for the new kid, the Johnny-come-lately. Riley's own definition: "a new kid on the block who's swinging it."

Keith Sweat's "I Want Her" was a number 5 pop hit that year. Riley says he came up with it in 1987. Then his own group, Guy, and "Groove Me." Then Bobby Brown's "My Prerogative." Then, in 1991, Michael Jackson's Dangerous, produced by Jackson and Riley.

Hank Shocklee put the whole thing in one sentence: "What made it really New Jack Swing was the snare." It had to be "loud and obnoxious and not in tune with the rest of the stuff." Riley says Jackson told him the same thing in the studio: turn up the snare.

Hip-hop drums, R&B singin over 'em, and that snare crackin through the mix like a gunshot in a ballroom, man.

How loud's your snare gotta be before it's the WHOLE FUCKING SONG?

### Straight down

Here's the strange bit. The name's got swing in it. The study behind this bank swings at 64%. And Riley says he played "I Want Her" and the first Guy album "straight down," by hand, with no sequencer, and it "didn't have the off and on swing" he wanted.

So where'd the swing come from? The sources ain't settled it. Wikipedia points at sampled 808 rhythms. An engineer in the Red Bull oral history points at the swing function on the MPC Riley's crew used later.

*Pulse here. I set it at 62.5%, Shake 6, because the study says 64 and the box can't hit 64. That's the only number on this page I chose. The clap and the open hat are mine too: 42% of the bank, with the climb. If you can play it straight down like Riley, try Shake 1 and see if you miss it.*

### The treatise

Legge's appendix on the judgment explains the figure in one sentence. The trigram for strength is "directing that which denotes movement, and hence (the whole) is expressive of vigour."

Four whole lines under two broken: the kit is strong, and the movement on top is the builder's.

What in your beat is strength, and what's movement?

### Scots and a shout

The Average White Band came out of Dundee and the towns around it. Bonnie Bramlett is supposed to have said they were "too much for the average white man," and that was the name.

The guitar line is Hamish Stuart's. The first part of the horn tune is Roger Ball's. Stuart remembers writing it in a little house in the Hollywood Hills, the groove "a very James Brown kind of thing," played on one chord for a long time. MCA turned the album down. Atlantic took it, and Jerry Wexler wanted the lot. When they cut the track again in New York, Robbie McIntosh found a funkier drum part. Arif Mardin was at the desk, at Atlantic in New York and at Criteria in Miami: the same room, and the same producer, as Rock Steady.

There are no words. Nothin but the horns, and the title shouted a few times. Malcolm Duncan didn't want it out as a single: who'd play a "funk instrumental played by Scotsmen with no lyrics other than a shout"?

What does a song need if it ain't got words?  A job.

### The pieces

The UK single came out in July 1974 and didn't chart. The album came out that August.

On September 23, 1974, Robbie McIntosh died of a heroin overdose at a party in Los Angeles. Alan Gorrie overdosed too; Cher kept him conscious until help came. Steve Ferrone took the drum chair.

On February 22, 1975, "Pick Up the Pieces" topped the US pop chart. Stuart says the news came only five months after Robbie died, and it felt "a slightly hollow" kind of win. James Brown's band answered it with "Pick Up the Pieces, One by One," under the name AABB: the Above Average Black Band.

This bank's beat is McIntosh's. The kick on beats 1 and 3 and the + of 3, open hats lifting off the + of 1 and the + of 3, a closed hat sneakin in on the a of 2. Light on its feet, every bar, man.

What do you play when you lost your pieces?

The book's text puts the open hats in one place, and its drawing puts them in another. The research went with the drawing: the + of 1 and the + of 3. Stubblefield-style, it says, to keep it movin.

Who do you believe?

Your band quits in March and the session is in April. What do you stop worryin about?

### A new band by April

In March 1970 James Brown's band walked out on him. One account says it was over wages. Another says harsh treatment and stingy pay. He brought in a young Cincinnati band led by two brothers (Byrd helped find them, by one account), Bootsy Collins on bass and Catfish Collins on guitar. Jabo Starks stayed on drums, the one man from the old line-up still behind a kit.

On April 25 they played a show in Nashville and went straight to the studio. On the way Brown wrote the song on a paper bag. Collins remembers him tearing the bag in half. Two takes. Bootsy remembers asking about the mix, and Brown telling him: "Mix it? It's already mixed, son!"

The horns barely matter on this one. Wikipedia says the song "centers on the insistent riff" of the two brothers. The rest is Brown and Bobby Byrd callin back and forth, and Brown askin the band, out loud, should he take 'em to the bridge.

It came out that July in two parts, one each side of the 45. It went to 15 pop and 2 on the R&B chart.

Who in your band would even know if you took it to the bridge?

### Felt, not heard

Here's the thing about Jabo, man. The open hat lifts on the + of 1 and slams shut on 2. Lifts on the + of 3, shuts on 4. Every bar, all night, like a door swingin in a hot kitchen. Underneath it, four ghost notes the book says you feel more than you hear.

The book's text and its drawing argue about one of those ghosts. Ainsworth's measurements side with the drawing: the e of 3. That's what's on the card.

How quiet can a note get before it stops counting?

### The card on the table

His rule for this card was simple: a record's swing goes in only where somebody measured it. This is one of the few that got measured. Ainsworth's paper times every hit, and the late backbeat is in there too. B. couldn't build that one in. The box drags kick and snare together or not at all. So it sits in the also-try, a setting you dial when you want to hear what's missin.

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The book, checked against Ainsworth: kick, backbeat, both hats and the four ghosts.

The builder's own: the cowbell. 50% of the bank.

### Soul on the door

July 1967. Isaac Hayes was watching the news from Detroit after the 12th Street riot. Black residents had marked the buildings that were still standing, most of them Black-owned, with one word: soul. Hayes told NPR, as uDiscover quotes him, that "if you put 'soul' on the door of your business establishment," they wouldn't burn it. David Porter remembers Hayes bringin the title in.

Detroit to Memphis is a long way. The word made it.

Hayes and Porter wrote it and produced it at Stax. Porter credits "a little beat that \[drummer\] Al Jackson put on the track." Sam Moore shouted "Play it, Steve" in the middle of a take, at Steve Cropper, and they kept it.

Who's on Booker T.'s piano stool is an open question. Wikipedia lists him. uDiscover says he was away at college.

What word would you paint on your fathers door?

### Second place

It came out in September 1967 and went to 2 pop. Lulu's "To Sir With Love" held the top. On the R&B chart it ran first for seven weeks. Wikipedia calls it "the most successful Stax single to date upon its release." It won a Grammy. Eleven years later the Blues Brothers opened Saturday Night Live with it, and their single went to 14.

Listen to the kick, man. Two of 'em land on an a, the last little 16th before the beat, and each one leans the bar into the next. That's the book's whole case for this record, forward momentum, and you can hear it from the first bar. Al Jackson doin it with one foot, cool as Sunday.

Would you rather be the record that peaked at off down beow the edge of the charts, or the #1 that sucks corporate ballz?

### The fourth line

The fourth line of Kwâi gives good advice to a man who won't take it: "But though he hear these words, he will not believe them."

What have you been told about your own feat that you didn't believe?

### Sigma Sound

In Philadelphia in the early 1970s, Kenny Gamble and Leon Huff ran Philadelphia International Records out of Sigma Sound. The house band was MFSB. At the bottom of it was a rhythm section of three, Ronnie Baker, Norman Harris and Earl Young.

Young played on the O'Jays and on Harold Melvin and the Blue Notes. "Backstabbers." "For The Love Of Money." "The Love I Lost," 1973. Later he started the Trammps, and they had "Disco Inferno."

His own account is short. "I actually kind of created disco."

### Who started it

Nobody agrees on a first record. Some people point at "The Love I Lost." A guest on NPR who has heard that argument says "there's not really one song that you can point to." Wikipedia says Young is "seen as the inventor" of the disco style of drumming on it.

The pattern itself is easy to say: four kicks a bar, snare on 2 and 4, 8th-note hats on top. The hat is the part people credit to him. Wikipedia, citing a Dutch radio interview, calls him the first drummer to make heavy, distinctive use of it all the way through a song. DJs liked that, because they could hear the cymbal when they cued the record up.

### Twenty-nine seconds

Billie Jean came out on January 3, 1983, the second single from Thriller. Seven weeks at the top of the pop chart, nine on R&B, number one in Britain.

Quincy Jones wanted to cut the intro, 29 seconds of drums and bass before anybody sings. Jackson wouldn't. Jones gave in when Jackson told him the intro made him want to dance.

That's you, chaser. Half a minute of kick, snare and hat, and you're already up. You knew before he sang a word.

Jones also wanted to change the title. Jackson said no to that too, and they didn't speak for days.

What would you fight your producer to keep? Your shoes? His shoes?

### The platform

Jones told Bruce Swedien he wanted a drum sound with "sonic personality," something nobody had heard. Swedien built a platform for the kit, and put a flat piece of wood between the snare and the hi-hat. Leon "Ndugu" Chancler played it. The archive pairs him with a LinnDrum. Louis Johnson played the bass part on every bass guitar he owned before they kept one. Swedien mixed the song 91 times. They used the second mix.

Kurt Cobain said he was trying to write "the ultimate pop song." He named the Pixies as the band in his head. The title came off a wall: Kathleen Hanna wrote a line about Kurt and teen spirit, and he asked her later if he could use it. It's the only song on Nevermind credited to all three of them.

Then the chorus. Hats half open on every beat, the kick pilin up on the a of 1, the + of 3, the a of 3, snare crackin on 2 and doublin back on the a of 2 and the e of 3. It's a disco beat with its shirt torn, man, played loud enough to shake the room.

Is it still disco if nobody dances to it like disco?

Heaven under a lake: the forty-third figure, Kwâi. Legge says Kwâi "denotes dispersion."

### The image

The waters of a marsh rising up over heaven, and a man who "bestows emolument on those below him, and dislikes allowing his gifts to accumulate (undispensed)."

### The record

"Apache" starts out as a guitar tune Jerry Lordan wrote in the late '50s. The Shadows take it to number one in the UK. In America it's a Danish guitarist, Jørgen Ingmann, number one in 1961.

Then 1972. Michael Viner, an exec at MGM Records, needs music for a B-movie called *The Thing With Two Heads*, and puts a studio band together to cut it. The band keeps going and makes an album, *Bongo Rock*, out in 1973, with Jim Gordon on drums and King Errisson on bongos. Their "Apache" has a long percussion break right in the middle. It isn't a hit. It sits in the racks.

Then the Bronx. The sources argue about the year, somewhere in the '70s. DJ Kool Herc gets two copies. Break on one deck, same break cued up on the other, and back, and back again, round and round so the drums never quit. He called it the merry-go-round, man. The record nobody bought, playin twice at once, for a floor full of kids who'd never heard of the movie or the band or the cat who wrote the tune, and Fender's account says "the place would jump." By 1981 the Sugarhill Gang are rappin over it and Grandmaster Flash is cuttin it into *The Adventures of Grandmaster Flash on the Wheels of Steel*. Rolling Stone, rememberin Viner, called his record "the cornerstone of hip-hop." The stone got laid in the Bronx, though, on Herc's two decks.

Is a record that never sold a failure, if it's still makin floors jump fifty years on?

### The broken line

Legge reads the one broken line on top of Kwâi as "the last remnant, cold and dark, of winter," about to give way to "the warm and bright days of the approaching summer."

*Pulse here. The bongos are mine. The transcription doesn't place them, and the one cheat sheet that did couldn't be checked, so I programmed them and marked them. Treat them as a sketch.*

### The card on the table

Same band, other side of the table. A Bristol crew sampled another of their tunes, "Last Bongo in Belgium," for a slow, heavy record, and that record is a bank on B.'s own card: half time, Flam, every hit dragging its feet.

One band, both cards. This one's here 'cause it made a floor jump. That one's there 'cause it makes a room sink.

Which room are you playing to tonight?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: kick, snare, 8th hats, ghost snares and the open hat, from Patrick Ainsworth's transcription of the break in "Microtiming in Early Funk" (2025), the study that measured where each hit really falls. Two bars, A and B.

The builder's own: the bongos. 43% of the bank.

### One question to carry

What's sittin in your phat stax?

What have you put away that's ready to come back out?

### The Warehouse

Frankie Knuckles was a disco DJ from the Bronx who moved to Chicago. The Warehouse opened in 1977 under Robert "Robbie" Williams, an after-hours club that ran till morning, and Knuckles was its first musical director. Its crowd was mostly gay Black and Latino men. He played records, and his own reel-to-reel edits of them.

On July 12, 1979, a Chicago ballgame was turned into Disco Demolition Night. The Warehouse kept playing it.

Late in 1982 Knuckles left for a new club, the Power Plant, and started putting drum machines into his sets. The Warehouse became the Music Box, with Ron Hardy behind the decks. WBMX had been running the Hot Mix 5 on the radio since 1981. In 1984 Jesse Saunders and Vince Lawrence put out "On and On," built on an 808. By 1987 Steve "Silk" Hurley's "Jack Your Body" was the first house record to top the British chart.

Who kept your music going when nobody else wanted it?

### The name, and the move

Knuckles told NPR where the name came from: "It's the House, that's everybody's nickname for the place." The Grammy timeline says the same, the style named after the Warehouse. Wikipedia says the accounts conflict, and Saunders says the word was already the local name for the music.

The dance has a name too. To jack is to move the torso forward and back in a ripple. A DJ on NPR put it plainer: "bend your knees and you pump your hips."

And that's the beat, man. Kick on every beat, the clap slappin 2 and 4, open hats hissin on the +, and the little closed ones on the e and the a draggin late, just a hair, just enough. Straight, it's a metronome. Shuffle those hats and the whole room starts to ripple from the knees up.

Does the room name the music?

### The card on the table

This is SuperTime's jack-house groove, a study of the style rather than any one record. The study puts the closed-hat shuffle at 56% and says that's the jack. Shake 2, at 57, is the nearest row the box has.

The builder's own: the shaker and the rim. 40% of the bank, with the climb.

### The top line

Legge's top line of Ming Î is a man who "had at first ascended to (the top of) the sky." Then the turn: "his future shall be to go into the earth."

Where does a sound from the future go, once the future shows up?

### Musicland

Donna Summer, Giorgio Moroder and Pete Bellotte made it at Musicland in Munich. Juergen Koppers engineered. The Moog was borrowed from the composer Eberhard Schoener, and Robby Wedel ran it. The Moog kept drifting out of tune, so they cut the record in bursts of 20 or 30 seconds. The whole thing took two or three hours.

The kick is a fight between sources. Wikipedia says Keith Forsey played it on a real kit, the only part not played by a machine. Sound On Sound has Wedel making the bass drum on the Moog. This page won't pick. Forsey's the drummer from the hat story on bank 18.

The album, *I Remember Yesterday*, walked through the decades one song at a time, and this was the last track: the future. Bellotte said the idea came from Anthony Powell's novels, *A Dance to the Music of Time*.

Did they know which part thier day was the future?

### The sound of the future

It came out on July 2, 1977, on Casablanca, as the B-side. Two months later they flipped it. Britain had it on top for four weeks. In America it got to 6.

Bowie told the story of Brian Eno running in with it in Berlin, calling it "the sound of the future." Sound On Sound has Eno saying it would "change the sound of club music for the next 15 years." Wikipedia credits it as the first record to put looping synths over a steady four-on-the-floor kick and an offbeat hat. MusicRadar says "arguably."

Kick, hiss, kick, hiss. That's all of it, man, eight hits a bar, the barest classic on the card. Nothin on the backbeat at all. The Moog bass boilin underneath, Donna floatin up over the top, and down at the bottom that dumb, perfect thump that never once changes its mind. Fifty years of dance floors are standin on it.

What's that thump in your bottom?

### The card on the table

The research flagged this one as thin. No drum book writes it out; a magazine and an encyclopedia describe a kick on every beat and a hat on the off-beat, and that's all. The tempo, 126.5, is MusicRadar's.

*Pulse here. 63% of this bank is me, more than any other bank on the card. The sources hand over a kick and a hat. The snare, the open hat, the noise tick and the clap are me, and all four go quiet at 17. Mute those four tracks and what's left is everything anybody wrote down about the drums.*

### The second line

Line two of Tâ Khû, in Legge, is "a carriage with the strap under it removed." Take the strap out and the carriage stays where it is.

Something came out of this record too, by Sound On Sound's account: the snare. What was left on the backbeat was the kick and the handclaps. It didn't stop. It rolled.

What could you pull out of your beat and still have it roll?

### Thirty dollars

Afrika Bambaataa and the Soulsonic Force made it with Arthur Baker producing and John Robie on keys. Tom Silverman put it out on Tommy Boy.

The 808 came from a man named Joe, who answered an ad in the Village Voice: "Man with drum machine, $30 a session." Wikipedia says twenty. They programmed a beat on it from Kraftwerk's "Numbers," and played the tune of "Trans-Europe Express" over the top. Silverman agreed to pay Kraftwerk a dollar for every record sold. They made it at Intergalactic Studio in New York, in about thirty hours.

Thirty bucks, man. Some cat named Joe walks in off a classified ad with a box under his arm, and by the time he packs up, electro's got its first beat. Kick bouncin off the + of 2 and the + of 3, the hats ratchetin through every 16th, that cowbell clankin on the offbeat, and the claps snappin where a snare should be.

What's the cheapest thing that ever changed your music?

### Year zero

It came out in 1982; the sources don't agree on the month. It got to 48 on the pop chart, 4 on the soul chart and 3 on the dance chart, and went gold by October. One critic called it "year zero of the new dance music." Native Instruments calls it "the first electro track." Wikipedia says the style only got the name electro later.

Could anybody in the room that year have told you it was the first of anything?

### One question to carry

What's the one sound you'd pay a stranger thirty dollars to bring round?

A warning, or a comfort?

### The cable

New Order cut it in 1982 at Britannia Row, on an Oberheim DMX. Stephen Morris tried it on real drums first, and that didn't work. The DMX kick went down at three volumes, which Morris lists as "loud, loud, loud." They played the kick back through a monitor to get the room on it.

The idea, MusicRadar says, was a track the machines could play as the encore while the band had a beer.

Then the accident. Bernard Sumner, to GQ in 2012: "He ripped the power cable out and lost all the drum programming." Morris takes the blame: "all my fault." Peter Hook says the cord was short and the machine was sitting on the floor. A day's programming, gone. They built it again.

And that kick, man. Four on the floor, steady as a clock, and then halfway through bars 1 and 3 it loses it, sixteenths stammerin off the 3, eight in a row into the bar line, then back on the beat like nothin happened. Loud, loud, loud. You feel it in your teeth.

The box plays the encore, chaser. That was the whole plan. B.'s already got his feet up.

Who's playin your encore? Fuck encores anyway...

### Borrowed, and sold at a loss

MusicRadar's headline says the riff was "stolen" from a Donna Summer B-side, and Wikipedia names it: "Our Love." That's the same Donna Summer as bank 25. Reverb hears the arrangement in Klein & M.B.O.'s "Dirty Talk" instead, and Morris shrugs it off: "people have been doing it for ages."

Factory put it out as a 12-inch on March 7, 1983: FAC 73, seven and a half minutes long. Peter Saville's sleeve was cut like a floppy disk, and cost so much that Factory lost money on every copy. Tony Wilson put the loss at 5p a sleeve; MusicRadar says 10p. It's often called the best-selling 12-inch single there is. It got to 12 in Britain, then 9 later that year, and a remix got to 3 in 1988.

Would you pay to put out a record?

### Vigour

Legge's sixth appendix gives the reason Tâ Kwang hands on to the next figure: "Things cannot remain for ever (simply) in the state of vigour."

Motorik never heard that. It stays at full stride for ten minutes and doesn't turn.

### Four nights in Hamburg

Michael Rother and Klaus Dinger had both come out of an early Kraftwerk, in 1971. Conny Plank sat between them as what Wikipedia calls a "mediator." They made the first Neu! record in four nights in December 1971 at Star Studio in Hamburg, and Brain put it out in 1972.

"Hallogallo" opens side one and runs ten minutes. The title plays on Halligalli, German slang for a wild party. Q magazine called the beat "krautrock's defining relentless rhythm." Bowie was a fan, and asked Rother onto "Heroes." It never happened.

Dinger said he never used the word motorik. He called the beat "lange gerade" or "endlose gerade": the long straight, the endless straight. Later, for a solo record in 1985, he called it the "Apache beat." Wikipedia flags that one as needing a source. Same name as bank 23, different beat.

What's the longest you've ever let one idea run?

### The road

Kick on 1, on 3, and once more on the + of 3, like a foot pushin the pedal back down. Snare on 2 and 4. Eight hats a bar goin by like the white lines. That's it, man. No fill, no turn, no exit. Just the road and the engine and the next bar comin up exactly like the last one, for ten minutes, till your head stops countin and starts floatin.

### The record

It's 1972. Lyn Collins cuts "Think (About It)" for James Brown's label. Brown wrote it and produced it, his band the J.B.'s played it, and Jabo Starks is on the drums. Partway through, the band drops to the drums and somebody shouts. That's the "Yeah! Woo!" break. The sources don't say who shouted.

In 1988 Rob Base and DJ E-Z Rock build "It Takes Two" almost entirely out of pieces of it, and it goes platinum. A few years later hardcore and then jungle producers speed it up and slice it until the shouts turn into "squawks and tics." Origin Unknown, Goldie, 1993. WhoSampled counts it at least 3,017 times, the second most sampled record there is, behind only the Amen.

One writer says Brown was known for "taking credit for the musical contributions from his band and singers," and that "we'll probably never truly know" how much of the song was Lyn's.

Three thousand records later, whose beat is it, man?

### The dragons

Legge gives Khien a seventh note, for when every line comes up strong: "If the host of dragons (thus) appearing were to divest themselves of their heads, there would be good fortune."

A whole break, every line strong, and the luck's in takin the heads off. Cut it at the neck. Throw the pieces back in a different order, faster than anybody ever played 'em, till the drummer wouldn't know his own break and the floor can't stop movin. That's jungle, man. That's the chop.

*Pulse here. The chops are mine, every one. No source gives a standard way to cut this break, so I cut it and marked it. 41% of this bank. Cut it your own way; there isn't a right one.*

### Two dancers

A music theorist in Florida watched a drum and bass floor. For some of the dancers, he wrote, "the night (or morning, as it may be) is still young," and they ride the fast drums, "twice as fast as those more exhausted dancers" who move to the slow pulse underneath. Same record. And nothing stops either crowd from "changing their perspective at any given moment."

Which one are you right now? When'd you last switch?

### The card on the table

The dusty card had jungle on it once, a chopped Amen at 170, bank 30, first round. A later pass wrote it up as "Forward — chopped Amen at 170, constantly destabilised; the break never sits still," and pulled it. Halftime drum and bass took its chair: written at 170, felt at 85, one snare a bar.

The Amen was already spoken for, slowed way down on the slow card, so this one couldn't use it. That's how the Think break ended up here.

The old man in that alley makes drum and bass. Which card's he reachin for at three in the mornin?

### What the card holds, and what it don't

The record: all six tracks, from Patrick Ainsworth's transcription of bar 23 of the break. Kick, snare, ghost snares, 8th hats with the open one on the + of 4, tambourine on every 16th.

The builder's own: every chop, the climb and the variations. 41% of the bank.

What it can't do: Ainsworth measured the snare on 4 coming in ahead of the beat and louder, pulling the loop round into the next bar. The card plays it on the beat.

### One question to carry

A shout and a few bars of drums, tucked in the middle of somebody else's song, turned into a whole kind of music. Which part of your own track would you have cut?

The groove has no snare at all. It's toms, a feathered kick and the hat foot. So the snare line is broken, the press roll is broken, and the other four are whole. Fire below, wind above: the thirty-seventh figure, Kiâ Zăn. Legge says the name "simply means 'a household,' or 'the members of a family.'"

### The household

Louis Prima wrote it and recorded it with his New Orleans Gang in 1936. In July 1937, in Hollywood, Benny Goodman's band records Jimmy Mundy's arrangement, and it runs 8 minutes 43 seconds, both sides of a 12-inch 78, at a time when a band side was three minutes. Mundy folded in a Chu Berry tune, "Christopher Columbus." The singer Helen Ward's story is that it grew because one night Gene Krupa "just refused to stop drumming."

January 16, 1938. Carnegie Hall, the first time a swing band ever played there. Sing, Sing, Sing closes the night, a "killer diller" meant to get the crowd on its feet. Krupa's toms poundin, the horns screamin, and a hall that sat politely at the start of the night is with 'em now. Goodman finishes his solo and, outta nowhere, hands the next one to the piano player, Jess Stacy. And Stacy plays soft, man. He said later he was glad nobody told him first, or he'd have got nervous and "screwed it up." NPR: "Jess Stacy steals the show with his piano."

Legge's image for the household is wind coming up out of fire. The superior man in it orders his conduct "so that it is uniformly consistent." That's the floor tom. Every 8th, the whole tune, never lettin up, the one thing in the house everybody else can lean on.

Who holds your band together? Sniffing glue?

### Play it quiet

The book's note on this beat says the trick is playing the quiet strokes quiet, not the loud ones loud.

At 17 the card goes quieter still. The toms go, everything goes, except a feathered kick on 1 and the hat foot on every beat. Seven hits. The figure turns to ䷣, Ming Î, the sun gone down under the earth, what Legge calls "Intelligence Wounded." His image for it: the superior man "shows his intelligence by keeping it obscured."

Then at 20 the snare that was never in the groove comes in with a press roll, Rolls on, after Krupa's own.

which one carries the tune, the roll or the rush?

The dusty card swings on Trip 5 too. Same button, same row. Over there it's half-time shuffles, every + sinking into the last triplet like a boot in mud. Over here it's 216 and a big band running for the door.

Same setting. So where's the lean, in the box or in the hands?

This is the last bank on the card, and the fastest. After this, it's your half.

| Push | The e, the + and the a all move late. | Only as an "also try" |

How late is late depends on the row and the tempo, so each play page gives it in milliseconds for that bank. Feel stays on Tight everywhere, except Rolls at slot 20.

### Linear

Linear stays off on every bank here. If you want to hear what it does, try it on two banks. On 25, I Feel Love, the classic loses nothing, because the kick and the hat never meet. On 18, Disco, or 22, Smells Like Teen Spirit, it empties Hihat 1 completely, because every hat there lands on a kick or a snare.

### The two hats

Hihat 1 carries the time on most banks: the closed hat. Hihat 2 carries the open hat, and it's the track Rolls works on at 20. A few banks hand them other jobs. On 18 Hihat 1 is the pedal hat. On 31 it's the snare played on its hoop. On 32 it's the hat foot, and Hihat 2 is a snare press roll.

### The card files

Each bank is one PatternBank file, in each half of the card. For slots 1 to 32, bank N is file N minus 1, so bank 1 is PatternBank000 and bank 32 is PatternBank031. For slots 33 to 64, add 31: bank 1 is PatternBank032, bank 32 is PatternBank063. Every play page names its two files at the bottom. Keep a copy of a file before you change it.

### Glossary

- **Bank:** one beat on the card, with 24 patterns per track.
- **Slot:** where a track's knob sits, 1 to 24.
- **Classic:** the slot where a track plays its full home groove. All six on their classic is the bank as the record has it.
- **Build-up, drop, climb, peak:** slots 1 to 16, 17, 18 and 19, and 20. See The arc.
- **Second section:** a part of the record that isn't the main groove, like a verse or an intro. Where one is written down, it lives at 18.
- **Own, the builder's:** a hit or a track that isn't on the record. The builder added it.
- **17 on a sounds table:** the track is silent in the source, so its classic is the quiet slot.
- **20 (module limit):** the track's home groove already fills every step it can, so it sits at the peak.
- **e, +, a:** the three steps between beats. Say "one e and a."
- **Backbeat:** the snare on 2 and 4.
- **Four on the floor:** a kick on every beat.
- **Ghost note:** a quiet hit between the loud ones. On this box it's only quiet if the sound is.
- **Rimshot:** the stick hits the head and the rim at once. Loud and sharp.
- **Hoop:** the metal ring around a drum. Playing on it gives a click.
- **Feathered kick:** a kick played so soft it's felt more than heard.
- **Study:** a pattern written to show a style, not copied off one record.
- **Figure, line:** the I Ching drawing at the top of each story page. A whole line is a track the record plays; a broken line is the builder's.
- **The slow card:** the other card, sixty-four banks that lean back. Some story pages point to it.

### Things still to test

- The Rolls counts at 20 are worked out from how the module is meant to behave, not heard off the box.
- The millisecond figures are worked out from the settings, not measured off the box.
- Whether an open hat on Hihat 2 gets cut short by the closed hat on Hihat 1, the way a real pair of hats closes.
- Bank 3 changes Shake row at 18. A Shuffle change lands on the next beat; check it doesn't hiccup.
- Whether Trip 5 holds a clean shuffle at 178 and 216.
- Ghost notes need quiet sounds. Check your kit has some soft enough.

### The side package

The manual comes with a zip, `Chasing-the-Beat-Package.zip`, downloaded from the same place. Its home is github.com/stolperbeats/stolperbeats-forward-pack, with a copy kept for good on the Internet Archive.

- **Card Files - Slots 1-32** and **Card Files - Slots 33-64.** The same 32 banks twice. Pick the half of your card you don't mind replacing, and use that folder only.
- **AI-Kit.** A folder for an AI agent. It teaches the method behind the card: the 24-slot arc, the file format, the timing, Blue Monday traced slot by slot on all six tracks, and a page of facts for every bank. Small Python tools check the work.
- **README, CREDITS and LICENSE.**

**Loading a half.** Back up the SD card first. Copy the 32 files from one folder to the top level of the card. Change nothing else on it, not the config, state, envelope or MIDI files. Eject, power up, press play, then stop once; the module saves its state on stop. If those bank slots were switched off, hold a Bank button for 2 seconds to get into Bank Edit and turn them on.

**Using the kit.** Open an AI agent that can read a folder of files, hand it `AI-Kit`, and tell it to read `START-HERE.md` first. Then ask for a bank: a style, a record, a tempo. "A UK garage two-step at 132, the way these were built." Or steer one: "make bank 21 more like bank 24," "less busy at 18." It hands back a full PatternBank file, a note per track on what it did and why, the home setting to dial in, and an OWN label on every hit it made up. Hits you give it get labelled USER. It asks which slot the file's for and warns you that copying it over replaces that bank. It never touches your card. You do that, after a backup. It's been tested with Claude.
